# RED SEA FRESHWATER PROJECT

## Proposal for the Closure of the Bab el-Mandeb at Perim Island and Progressive Conversion of the Red Sea into a Managed Freshwater Reservoir

### Executive Summary

This proposal examines the construction of a major hydraulic barrier across the Bab el-Mandeb Strait at Perim Island, followed by the progressive desalination and freshwater conversion of the Red Sea.

The project would represent one of the largest hydraulic-engineering projects ever attempted.

The basic proposition is straightforward:

1. Construct a controlled barrier across the Perim Narrows.
2. Separate the Red Sea from the Gulf of Aden.
3. Prevent uncontrolled seawater exchange.
4. Establish a massive seawater desalination system around the Red Sea basin.
5. Remove freshwater continuously while concentrating and managing the remaining saltwater.
6. Use renewable and nuclear electricity to operate the desalination system.
7. Develop regional freshwater transmission infrastructure.
8. Ultimately operate the Red Sea as a managed freshwater or low-salinity reservoir.

The project is technically conceivable in individual components but **not presently demonstrated as an integrated engineering undertaking**.

The principal obstacle is scale.

The Bab el-Mandeb is not a shallow lake entrance that can simply be dammed. The Perim Narrows contains a deep central channel, reported in oceanographic studies at roughly 200–300 m depth, while the surrounding Bab el-Mandeb system includes the approximately 137–160 m Hanish sill that controls much of the exchange.

Consequently, the project would require a combination of artificial islands, immersed or subsea structures, enormous rock/concrete quantities, navigation infrastructure, and potentially ship locks or a separate maritime bypass.

The Red Sea also loses approximately two metres of water per year to evaporation in some published estimates. This evaporation is precisely what currently makes the Red Sea one of the world's most saline marine basins and drives its distinctive circulation through Bab el-Mandeb.

Therefore, simply closing the strait would not make the Red Sea freshwater.

It would initially make the Red Sea **saltier**.

The proposed project consequently treats the Red Sea as a gigantic desalination reservoir rather than as a naturally freshwater lake.

The objective is to create a new continental-scale freshwater system for the Red Sea region.

The proposed system would provide:

**dam + artificial lake + desalination plant + power station + water grid.**

# 2. The Geographical Concept

The Red Sea is approximately 2,000 km long and roughly 200–300 km wide in much of its main basin, with very deep central sections. The Bab el-Mandeb provides its connection with the Gulf of Aden and Indian Ocean.

The proposed closure point is the **Perim Narrows**.

Perim Island already divides the Bab el-Mandeb into separate passages.

The important engineering fact is that the narrowest part is not necessarily the shallowest.

Published oceanographic measurements describe the Perim Narrows as approximately 20–25 km wide with a central depth of roughly 200–300 m, whereas the Hanish sill farther north is shallower and has a controlling depth around 137–160 m depending on the bathymetric dataset.

This means the project should **not** initially be designed as a conventional earth dam.

It should be designed as a **multi-element maritime closure system**.

# 3. The Perim Hydraulic Barrier

The primary structure would consist of:

### A. Western closure

A large artificial island/caisson system would extend from the African side toward Perim.

### B. Perim Island complex

Perim would become the central engineering platform.

The island would be expanded substantially through:

* rock fill;
* precast caissons;
* concrete gravity structures;
* armourstone;
* dredged material;
* artificial reef protection zones.

### C. Eastern closure

A corresponding structure would connect Perim to the Arabian/Yemeni side.

### D. Deep-channel closure

The deepest central navigation channel would require the principal subsea barrier.

A plausible first-generation design would therefore use:

**artificial islands → immersed caissons → deep foundation → rock/concrete armour → hydraulic gates.**

The final structure should not be assumed to be a single monolithic wall.

# 5. Why Closing the Red Sea Does Not Immediately Produce Freshwater

This is the most important point in the proposal.

The Red Sea is already subject to very high evaporation.

The present oceanographic system involves surface inflow from the Gulf of Aden and a dense saline outflow from the Red Sea. The circulation is strongly influenced by evaporation and seasonal monsoon conditions.

If the connection were closed:

**evaporation would continue.**

But:

**Indian Ocean replacement water would stop.**

The Red Sea would therefore lose water while retaining its dissolved salts.

Its salinity would rise.

The project consequently requires continuous desalination.

# 8. Scale of the Problem

The Red Sea's area is enormous.

At approximately 400,000+ km², an evaporation rate on the order of 2 m/year corresponds to a potential natural freshwater loss approaching:

**~800 km³/year.**

That is approximately:

**800 billion cubic metres of water per year.**

This does not mean the project would necessarily have to desalinate 800 km³/year immediately, because the objective could initially be to establish a controlled water balance rather than eliminate all evaporation.

But it illustrates the fundamental scale.

A desalination system producing:

**100 km³/year**

would produce approximately:

**274 million m³/day.**

That is several orders of magnitude beyond an individual desalination plant.

The project therefore becomes a **continental water industry** rather than a conventional utility project.

# 15. The Freshwater Reservoir

This is substantially more realistic.

The second configuration should therefore be the initial engineering target.

# 21. Salinity Model

The principal conservation equation is:

$$
\frac{d(MS)}{dt}
=
S_{in}Q_{in}
-
S_{out}Q_{out}
-
S_fQ_f
+
S_{river}Q_{river}
$$

where:

* \(M\) = basin water mass;
* \(S\) = salinity;
* \(Q_f\) = freshwater extraction;
* \(Q_{in}\) = ocean inflow;
* \(Q_{out}\) = ocean outflow.

After closure:

$$
Q_{in}\approx Q_{out}\approx0
$$

so the principal system becomes:

$$
\frac{d(MS)}{dt}
\approx
-S_fQ_f
$$

with evaporation removing water but not salt.

The digital twin would therefore continuously calculate the changing concentration of the entire basin.

$$
\frac{dV}{dt}
=

# 29. Brine Disposal Principle

The proposal should explicitly reject the simplistic strategy:

**"pump all the brine back into the Red Sea."**

At sufficiently large scale, that simply moves the salt problem from the desalination plant to the basin.

The preferred hierarchy is:

1. mineral recovery;
2. salt crystallisation;
3. industrial utilisation;
4. controlled residual discharge;
5. only as a last resort, long-term basin concentration.

Existing research indicates that the Red Sea's large-scale desalination capacity can have relatively small basin-wide salinity effects compared with natural evaporation under some scenarios, but that does not remove the requirement for carefully engineered outfalls and local ecological protection.

# 60. Final Recommendation

The **Perim closure concept merits investigation as a long-range hydraulic and water-infrastructure study**, but the proposition should not currently be treated as a conventional dam-and-reservoir project.

The engineering challenge is not simply constructing a barrier.

It is controlling a basin approximately 2,000 km long whose present circulation is governed by evaporation, salinity, temperature, monsoon forcing and a complex deep-water exchange through Bab el-Mandeb.

The central engineering proposition should therefore be:

> **Do not try to make the Red Sea freshwater first. Make the Red Sea controllable first.**

Once the hydraulic boundary is understood and controllable, desalination can progressively convert the basin's enormous water inventory into a strategically managed freshwater resource.

The resulting programme would be one of the largest combinations of **civil engineering, desalination, nuclear and renewable energy, marine science, water infrastructure and computational modelling** ever attempted.

Its first construction site should therefore not be Perim.

It should be the **digital Red Sea**.

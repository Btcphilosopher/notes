Kantian epistemology begins from the claim that human knowledge is neither a mere copy of an independently existing world nor something constructed entirely from experience, but arises through the interaction of sensibility and understanding. In the Critique of Pure Reason, Immanuel Kant argues that experience provides the raw material of knowledge, while the mind actively structures that material through the a priori forms of intuition, space and time, and the categories of the understanding, such as causality, substance, and unity. Consequently, we can know objects only as they appear to us—phenomena—rather than things as they might exist independently of our mode of cognition—noumena or “things in themselves.” Kant therefore attempts to reconcile empiricism and rationalism: knowledge requires experience, but experience itself is intelligible only because the mind contributes universal structures that organise it. His central epistemological insight is thus that the conditions under which we can experience objects are simultaneously conditions imposed by the knowing subject.
